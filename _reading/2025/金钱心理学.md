## Introduction: The Greatest Show On Earth

“人类涉及金钱的行为是地球上最伟大的表演之一。”

在引言中，作者提出了本书的重要前提：理财的成功与智商关系不大，而与行为方式关系很大。

> The premise of this book is that doing well with money has a little to do with how smart you are and a lot to do with how you behave.

他举了两个人的例子：Ronald Read 和 Richard Fuscone。一个普通的蓝领工人通过节俭投资积累了数百万财富，而一位受过高等教育的金融精英却因过度负债在危机中破产。这说明 1）理财的成功很多时候靠运气，而非智力和努力；2）这是一项软技能，行为比知识更重要，作者称之为“金钱心理学”。

> The fact that Ronald Read can coexist with Richard Fuscone has two explanations. One, **financial outcomes are driven by luck, independent of intelligence and effort**. That’s true to some extent, and this book will discuss it in further detail. Or, two (and I think more common), that **financial success is not a hard science. It’s a soft skill, where how you behave is more important than what you know**.

尽管人类在各领域不断进步，但在理财方面，并没有明显变得更优秀。这可能是因为我们倾向于用研究物理的方式来学习理财，而忽视了其深受心理因素影响的本质。作者在研究和撰写金融危机时发现，没人能准确解释其成因或应对之道。深入研究后，他意识到，理解金融危机或许需要更多关注心理和历史，而非仅限于金融本身：

> The more I studied and wrote about the financial crisis, the more I realized that you could understand it better through the lenses of psychology and history, not finance.

>  To grasp why people bury themselves in debt you don’t need to study **interest rates**; you need to study **the history of greed, insecurity, and optimism**. To get why investors sell out at the bottom of a bear market you don’t need to study **the math of expected future returns**; you need to think about **the agony of looking at your family and wondering if your investments are imperiling their future**. 

> I love Voltaire’s observation that "History never repeats itself; man always does." It applies so well to how we behave with money.


## 1. No One’s Crazy

人们在金钱上的行为可能看似疯狂，但实则各有合理性。这是因为 1）每个人的成长经历、环境和运气不同，对金钱的认知也不同；2）理财是相对新的概念，许多看似疯狂的行为可能只是视角不同。

一、个人经历影响理财行为：

> Everyone has their own unique experience with **how the world works**. And what you’ve experienced is more compelling than what you learn second-hand. So all of us—you, me, everyone—go through life anchored to a set of views about **how money works** that vary wildly from person to person. <u>What seems crazy to you might make sense to me.</u>

举例子：
1. 出身贫困 vs 出身富有 --> 对风险和回报的看法不同
2. 通货膨胀时期长大 vs 物价稳定时期长大
3. 大萧条期间的股票经纪人 vs 90年代末美国经济发展荣光中的技术工人
4. 30年没有经历过经济衰退的澳大利亚人 vs 美国人
5. ...数不胜数的例子

二、亲身经历具有局限性：

> We all think we **know** how the world works. But we’ve all only **experienced** a tiny sliver of it.

举例子：
- 大萧条对美国社会产生了深远影响，许多人一生都未能摆脱其阴影。然而，肯尼迪家族在大萧条期间并未受影响，反而财富增加，生活更加优渥。肯尼迪本人直到哈佛时期才真正意识到大萧条的影响。

> The economists wrote: "Our findings suggest that individual investors’ **willingness to bear risk** depends on personal history." Not intelligence, or education, or sophistication. Just the dumb luck of **when and where you were born**.

> Every decision people make with money is justified by taking the information they have at the moment and plugging it into their unique mental model of how the world works.

二、理财是相对新的概念：

> Another important point that helps explain why money decisions are so difficult, and why there is so much misbehavior, is to recognize how new this topic is.

> We all do crazy stuff with money, because we’re all relatively new to this game and what looks crazy to you might make sense to me.



## 2. Luck & Risk

> Bill Gates experienced one in a million luck by ending up at Lakeside. Kent Evans experienced one in a million risk by never getting to finish what he and Gates set out to achieve. 

> Luck and risk are both the reality that every outcome in life is guided by forces other than individual effort. 

> When judging others, attributing success to luck makes you look jealous and mean, even if we know it exists. And when judging yourself, attributing success to luck can be too demoralizing to accept.

> Everything worth pursuing has less than 100% odds of succeeding, and risk is just what happens when you end up on the unfortunate side of that equation.

> We similarly think Mark Zuckerberg is a genius for turning down Yahoo!’s 2006 $1 billion offer to buy his company. He saw the future and stuck to his guns. But people criticize Yahoo! with as much passion for turning down its own big buyout offer from Microsoft—those fools should have cashed out while they could! What is the lesson for entrepreneurs here? I have no idea, because risk and luck are so hard to pin down.

> The line between “inspiringly bold” and “foolishly reckless” can be a millimeter thick and only visible with hindsight.

> The difficulty in identifying what is luck, what is skill, and what is risk is one of the biggest problems we face when trying to learn about the best way to manage money.

> Studying a specific person can be dangerous because we tend to study extreme examples—the billionaires, the CEOs, or the massive failures that dominate the news—and extreme examples are often the least applicable to other situations, given their complexity. 

> When things are going extremely well, realize it’s not as good as you think. You are not invincible, and if you acknowledge that luck brought you success then you have to believe in luck’s cousin, risk, which can turn your story around just as quickly.

> The trick when dealing with failure is arranging your financial life in a way that a bad investment here and a missed financial goal there won’t wipe you out so you can keep playing until the odds fall in your favor.

> There is no reason to risk what you have and need for what you don’t have and don’t need.

> Few of us will ever have $100 million, as Gupta or Madoff did. But a measurable percentage of those reading this book will, at some point in their life, earn a salary or have a sum of money sufficient to cover every reasonable thing they need and a lot of what they want.

> 1. The hardest financial skill is getting the goalpost to stop moving.

> This can be as innocent as burning out at work or a risky investment allocation you can’t maintain. On the other end there’s Rajat Guptas and Bernie Madoffs in the world, who resort to stealing because every dollar is worth reaching for regardless of consequence.


>  Reputation is invaluable.
   Freedom and independence are invaluable.
   Family and friends are invaluable.
   Being loved by those who you want to love you is invaluable.
   Happiness is invaluable.
   And your best shot at keeping these things is knowing when it’s time to stop taking risks that might harm them. Knowing when you have enough.

## 4. Confounding Compounding

**复利的力量**：好的投资并非一定是追求超高回报率，因为超高的回报率很有可能是一次性的；相对来说，可持续的比较不错的回报率长期下来可以使你累积更多的财富，但人们时常容易低估甚至忽略这种力量。

> You can’t blame people for devoting all their effort—effort in what they learn and what they do—to trying to earn the highest investment returns. It intuitively seems like the best way to get rich.

> But good investing isn’t necessarily about earning the highest returns, because the highest returns tend to be one-off hits that can’t be repeated. It’s about **earning pretty good returns that you can stick with and which can be repeated for the longest period of time**. That’s when compounding runs wild.

**类比**：持续数万年的微小气候变化导致了地球上“冰河时期——间冰期”的轮回上演。

> The big takeaway from ice ages is that **you don’t need tremendous force to create tremendous results**.

> If something **compounds**—if a little growth serves as the fuel for future growth—a small starting base can lead to results so extraordinary they seem to defy logic.

**例子**：人们容易忽略的事实是巴菲特的投资时长持续了四分之三个世纪。
> As I write this Warren Buffett’s net worth is $84.5 billion. Of that, $84.2 billion was accumulated after his 50th birthday. $81.5 billion came after he qualified for Social Security, in his mid-60s.

## 5. Getting Wealthy vs. Staying Wealthy

**守财的技能**：发财和守财是两种不一样的技能，前者涉及到冒险和乐观，而后者是相反面——这要求我们谦逊、保持害怕，因为我们可能失去财富和得到财富一样快；要求我们勤俭、接受，因为我们过去的所得可能归功于运气，从而我们不能指望它可以被无限重复。


## 7. Freedom

**本章要点**：幸福感的关键在于拥有对生活的掌控感，追求财富是为了服务于这个目的，即赋予我们拥有掌控时间的自由。拼命追求财富的同时舍弃时间自由是一种舍本逐末的行为，我们不会因此变得更快乐。当下社会的脑力劳动方式使得我们对时间的掌控进一步被剥夺，解决这个问题的第一步是我们需要认识到快乐的关键因素究竟是什么。

**钱可以给带来的最大好处是让我们拥有掌控时间的自由**：幸福感的主导因素是拥有对生活的掌控感——什么时间、可以和什么人一起、做什么事情以及做多久的自由，因而这种自由是钱可以带给我们的最大好处。

> People want to become wealthier to make them happier. Happiness is a complicated subject because everyone’s different. But if there’s a common denominator in happiness—a universal fuel of joy—it’s that **people want to control their lives**.

> The ability to do what you want, when you want, with who you want, for as long as you want, is priceless. It is the highest dividend money pays.

**例子**：
- 一点小财富可以让你在生病的时候休假，而不用担心破产
- 再多一点的财富可以让你在被裁员之后等待一份好工作，而非被迫接受第一份得到的工作，这可能是改变人生的事情
- 六个月的紧急备用金可以让你不被老板吓唬住，因为你知道自己可以休假并找下一份工作而不会因此完蛋
- 更多一点的财富可以让你选择工资更少但工作时间更灵活的工作，你可以花更少的时间通勤，或是可以处理紧急的医疗状况而不用担心你将为此付出什么代价
- 你可以在想要的时候退休，而不是需要的时候

**获取财富的同时牺牲自己对时间的掌控会让人不快乐**：如果我们在用钱去购买更多更好的东西的同时，牺牲了自己对时间的掌控感，最好的情况也不过是这种好处和坏处会相互抵消。

> Part of what’s happened here is that we’ve used our greater wealth to buy bigger and better stuff. But we’ve simultaneously given up more **control over our time**. At best, those things cancel each other out.

**例子**：
- 作者曾经梦想成为一名投行工作者，因为他认为这可以使他挣到很多钱。他在上大学的时候如愿进了一家投行实习，这是一份有智力挑战、工资很高且感觉到自己重要的工作，但过多的工作时间让他产生了自己是老板的奴隶的感受，一个月后就辞职了
- 美国是世界上最富有的国家之一，但一份涉及140个国家、15万人参与的调查显示，美国人拥有比全球平均值更高的“忧虑感”和“压力”

**时代的改变使得我们的工作方式改变了**：随着社会的发展，越来越多的工作不再是在田间、车间的体力劳动，而是一些体力劳动和脑力劳动的结合，比如管理、策划和服务行业。因为这些工作可以随时随地进行，虽然我们在工作场合的时间更少了，但却像是24小时在工作而无法下班。和之前的世代相比，我们对时间的掌控被消除了；而对时间的掌控是幸福感的关键，因此我们变得更富有的同时并没有更快乐。

> More of us have jobs that look closer to Rockefeller than a typical 1950s manufacturing worker, which means our days don’t end when we clock out and leave the factory. **We’re constantly working in our heads, which means it feels like work never ends**.

> Compared to generations prior, control over your time has diminished. And since controlling your time is such a key happiness influencer, we shouldn’t be surprised that **people don’t feel much happier even though we are, on average, richer than ever**.

**例子**：
- 1870年，46%的工作是农业，35%是工业；在今天，38%的工作是管理，41%的工作是服务业

**什么让人们更快乐，什么不**：上面提到的现象不是一个容易解决的问题，但我们第一步应该承认是什么使得几乎所有人感到快乐、什么不会。一项针对1000名美国老人的调查显示，没人认为应该拼命工作、和他人攀比财富、按照未来预期收入选择工作；高质量的友情、参与比个体存在更伟大的事业、花时间和子女相处更重要。

> What do we do about that? It’s not an easy problem to solve, because everyone’s different. The first step is merely acknowledging what does, and does not, make almost everyone happy.

> In his book _30 Lessons for Living_, gerontologist `Karl Pillemer` interviewed a thousand elderly Americans looking for the **most important lessons they learned from decades of life experience**. He wrote:
- No one—not a single person out of a thousand—said that to be happy you should **try to work as hard as you can to make money to buy the things you want**.
- No one—not a single person—said it’s important to be at least **as wealthy as the people around you**, and if you have more than they do it’s **real success**.
- No one—not a single person—said you should choose your work based on your desired **future earning power**.

> What they did value were things like **quality friendships, being part of something bigger than themselves, and spending quality, unstructured time with their children**.


## 8. Main in the Car Paradox

**本章要点**：有一个关于炫富的悖论——豪车悖论，人希望通过炫富来获取他人的喜爱和尊重，结果却可能不如预期。我们可能会误以为自己渴望的是财富，但实际上我们渴望的是想象中的喜爱和尊重。（显露出的）财富不一定会赢得他人的喜爱和尊重，而保持谦逊、善良、富有同情心会。

**生词**：
- `aristocratic`: notably superior or excellent
- `fleets`: a group (as of ships, planes, or trucks) operated under unified control
- `gawk`: to gape or stare stupidly
- `horsepower`: effective power

**人们并不如你想象中的那样因为你的财富而仰慕你**

**例子**：

- 作者曾经做过泊车员，他经常看见一些人开着豪车——法拉利、兰博基尼、劳斯莱斯。这些车主看起来很享受他人惊呆了的目光，可能认为别人很仰慕他们；但实际上作者只是在幻想自己拥有这些豪车的感觉，而几乎不会注意到车主本人。

> When you see someone driving a nice car, you rarely think, “Wow, the guy driving that car is cool.” Instead, you think, “Wow, if _I_ had that car people would think _I’m_ cool.” Subconscious or not, this is how people think.

> There is a paradox here: people tend to want wealth to signal to others that they should be liked and admired. But in reality those other people often bypass admiring you, not because they don’t think wealth is admirable, but because they **use your wealth as a benchmark** for **their own desire to be liked and admired**.

**我们渴求财富可能是因为误以为财富可以赢得尊重**

> The letter I wrote after my son was born said, "You might think you want an expensive car, a fancy watch, and a huge house. But I’m telling you, you don’t. What you want is **respect and admiration** from other people, and you think having expensive stuff will bring it. It almost never does—especially from the people you want to respect and admire you."

**赢得尊重另有它法：谦逊、善良、富有同情心**

> It’s a subtle recognition that people generally aspire to be respected and admired by others, and using money to buy fancy things may bring less of it than you imagine. If respect and admiration are your goal, be careful how you seek it. **Humility, kindness, and empathy** will bring you more respect than horsepower ever will.

## 9. Wealth is What You Don't See

**本章要点**：财富是“看不见”的东西——那些没有被你用于购买豪车豪宅名包名表头等舱等而花出去的钱，那些账户里的钱、金融资产才是财富。它的价值在于可以为你提供选项、灵活度、增长，以致于到有一天你可以用它来购买比现在能买到的够好的东西。正因如此，我们很难看出哪些人拥有“财富”，也就很难从那些榜样身上学到如何变得富有。

**生词**：
- `mediocre`: of moderate or low quality, value, ability, or performance : ordinary, so-so
- `default`: to fail to fulfill a contract, agreement, or duty
- `morsel`: a small quantity
- `forgone`: to give up the enjoyment or advantage of : do without
- `notorious`: widely and unfavorably known
- `ingrained`: forming a part of the essence or inmost being
- `razor`: a keen-edged cutting instrument for shaving or cutting hair
- `insolvency`: the fact or state of being insolvent : inability to pay debts

**我们无法从外表判断一个人的财富状况，因为真正的财富是我们看不见的**

**例子**：
- 作者在 2000 年代中期洛杉矶做泊车员时，物质外表被高度重视，许多人倾其收入购买豪车，即便并不富有。Roger 便是其中一例，他因无力偿还车贷失去保时捷，却毫无羞愧，而洛杉矶有很多像他这样的人。

> Someone driving a $100,000 car might be wealthy. But the only data point you have about their wealth is that they have $100,000 less than they did before they bought the car (or $100,000 more in debt). That’s _all_ you know about them.

> Wealth is the nice cars not purchased. The diamonds not bought. The watches not worn, the clothes forgone and the first-class upgrade declined. Wealth is **financial assets that haven’t yet been converted into the stuff you see**.

**积累财富的唯一途径就是不要挥霍你拥有的钱**

**例子**：
- 蕾哈娜因为过度消费差点儿破产，因此她告了自己的理财顾问。理财顾回应：“Was it really necessary to tell her that **if you spend money on things, you will end up with the things and not the money**?”

> When most people say they want to be a millionaire, what they might actually mean is “I’d like to **spend a million dollars**.” And that is literally **the opposite of being a millionaire**.

> The only way to be wealthy is to **not spend** the money that you do have. It’s not just the only way to **accumulate wealth**; it’s the very **definition of wealth**.

**Rich 是现在的收入，而 wealth 是没有花出去的收入**

**类比**
- 一项针对美国人的研究表明，人们通常会误认为自己消耗的卡路里是实际消耗的4倍多，而且在运动后摄入的卡路里是实际消耗的2倍。得到收入就像运动后，你会认为自己已经付出了，是时候犒劳自己了，而你挥霍出去的钱就像你吃下去的那一餐，这本可以是你积攒下来的财富（减掉的肥）。

> But wealth is hidden. It’s income not spent. Wealth is an option not yet taken to buy something later. Its value lies in offering you **options, flexibility, and growth** to one day purchase more stuff than you could right now.

> The danger here is that I think most people, deep down, want to be wealthy. They want **freedom and flexibility**, which is what financial assets not yet spent can give you.

**因为财富不可见，所以我们更难通过模仿榜样来学习致富**

**例子**：
- Ronald Read 直到死后才成为许多人的理财榜样，生前就连认识他的那些人也不知道他有多富有。

> People are good at learning by imitation. But **the hidden nature of wealth** makes it hard to imitate others and learn from their ways.

> It’s difficult to learn from what **you can’t see**. Which helps explain why it’s so hard for many to build wealth.

> **The world is filled with people who look modest but are actually wealthy and people who look rich who live at the razor’s edge of insolvency.** Keep this in mind when quickly judging others’ success and setting your own goals.

## 10. Save Money

**本章要点**：在财富积累的过程中，存钱是唯一一件我们可以自己掌控的事情，而且它可以给我们带来更多无形的好处，比如拥有灵活度和拥有对时间的控制权，这在高度竞争高度紧密联系的当今社会尤为重要。

> Past a certain level of income people fall into three groups: Those who save, those who don’t think they **can save**, and those who don’t think they **need to save**. This is for the latter two.

**生词**：
- `shroud`: something that covers, screens, or guards
- `propensity`: an often intense natural inclination or preference
- `pounce`: to seize upon and make capital of something
- `leeway`: an allowable margin of freedom or variation : tolerance
- `niche`: a place, employment, status, or activity for which a person or thing is best fitted

**积累财富和储蓄率息息相关，而不是收入或投资回报率**：投资回报可以让我们变得有钱，但是投资策略是否仍然有效、还能有效多久、以及市场如何，永远充满不确定性；存钱和节俭是我们可以控制的事情，而且无论是过去还是现在都一样有效。

**类比**：
- 1970s，看起来地球上的石油要被用光了。然而最终并没有，其中最大的原因要归功于技术的进步，使得石油的利用效率大大提升。追求投资回报率就像找到更多的能源，并非我们能控制的；而存钱就像提高资源利用率，是我们可以控制并且始终有效的。

> The biggest reason we overcame the oil crisis is because we started building cars, factories, and homes that are more **energy efficient** than they used to be. 

> The world grew its "energy wealth" not by increasing the energy it had, but by decreasing the energy it needed.

> Personal **savings and frugality**—finance’s conservation and efficiency—are parts of the money equation that are more **in your control** and have a 100% chance of being as **effective** in the future as they are today.

> Wealth is just the accumulated leftovers after you spend what you take in. And since you can build wealth without a high income, but **have no chance of building wealth without a high savings rate**, it’s clear which one matters more.

**存钱关乎你的真正需求和虚荣心**：我们对存钱这件事情的控制权超出我们的想象，当收入达到一定程度后，我们其实是在为自己的虚荣心买单——告诉别人我们有（过）钱。我们越少在意别人的眼光、渴求拥有的就越少、省下的钱就越多。

> Past a certain level of income, what you need is just what sits below **your ego**.

> But spending **beyond a pretty low level of materialism** is mostly a reflection of **ego approaching income**, a way to spend money to show people that you have (or had) money.

> Think of it like this, and one of the most powerful ways to increase your savings isn’t to raise your income. It’s to **raise your humility**.

> When you define savings as **the gap between your ego and your income** you realize why many people with decent incomes save so little. It’s a daily struggle against instincts to extend your peacock feathers to their outermost limits and keep up with others doing the same.

> Savings can be created by spending less.
> You can spend less if you desire less.
> And you will desire less if you care less about what others think of you.

**没有具体理由，你也应该存钱**：存钱是为了对抗未来的不确定性，存的每一分钱都是让未来更有可能把握在自己手中。

> Only saving for a specific goal makes sense in a **predictable** world. But ours isn’t. Saving is a hedge against life’s inevitable ability to surprise the hell out of you **at the worst possible moment**.

> Every bit of savings is like taking a point in the **future** that would have been owned by someone else and **giving it back to yourself**.

**灵活度和对时间的掌控权在当今世界尤为重要**：储蓄给我们带来的灵活性和对时间的控制权是无价的，在赢者通吃、全球竞争的时代，诸如灵活性这样的软技能尤为宝贵，超越了智力能带来的优势。

**例子**（拥有灵活性的好处）：
- 你可以等待好的机会，无论是职业上的还是投资上的
- 必要的时候，更有机会学习新的技能
- 当你的竞争者可以做你做不到的事情的时候，你可以更从容；也有更多的余地去找到你自己的热爱和最适合的岗位
- 你可以更缓慢地从不同的角度思考生活，找到新的途径

> A question you should ask as the range of your competition expands is, "**How do I stand out?**" "I’m smart" is increasingly a bad answer to that question, because there are a lot of smart people in the world. 

> Intelligence is not a **reliable advantage** in a world that’s become as connected as ours has. But **flexibility** is.

> Having **more control over your time and options** is becoming one of the most valuable currencies in the world.
